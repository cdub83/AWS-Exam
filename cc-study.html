The CIA Triad

Confidentiality

  • Prevents unauthorized disclosure of information
  • Achieved via encryption, access controls, need-to-know
  • Breach example: a database leak of unencrypted SSNs

Integrity

  • Ensures data is accurate and unaltered except by authorized action
  • Achieved via hashing, digital signatures, version control
  • Breach example: an attacker silently modifies a bank balance

Availability

  • Ensures authorized users can access data/systems when needed
  • Achieved via redundancy, failover, DDoS protection, backups
  • Breach example: a DDoS attack takes a website offline
⚠ Exam Trap
Additional concepts often paired with CIA: Non-repudiation (a party cannot deny an action — e.g., digital signatures) and Authenticity (data/identity is genuine). Neither is part of the original CIA triad but both appear on the exam alongside it.
IAAA — Identification, Authentication, Authorization, Accounting

Identification

  • A CLAIM of identity — e.g., typing a username
  • Not yet proven — anyone could type any username

Authentication

  • PROVING the claimed identity is genuine
  • Factors: know (password), have (token), are (biometric), somewhere you are (location), something you do (behavior)

Authorization

  • Determines WHAT an authenticated user may do or access
  • Occurs only after successful authentication

Accounting (Auditing)

  • Logging/recording user actions for accountability
  • Supports investigations and non-repudiation
Security Control Categories & Types
⚠ Exam Trap
Know the two axes: Category (Technical, Administrative/Managerial, Physical) vs Type (Preventive, Detective, Corrective, Deterrent, Compensating, Directive). A camera is Physical + Detective. A policy is Administrative + Directive. A fence is Physical + Preventive/Deterrent.

Control Types

  • Preventive — stops an incident (firewall, lock)
  • Detective — identifies an incident (camera, audit log)
  • Corrective — restores after incident (backup restore, patch)
  • Deterrent — discourages attack (warning sign, guard)
  • Compensating — alternative when primary control can't be used
  • Directive — instructs behavior (policy, procedure)

Control Categories

  • Technical — firewalls, encryption, IDS/IPS, MFA
  • Administrative — policies, risk assessments, awareness training
  • Physical — locks, fences, cameras, mantraps, badge readers
Risk Management

Core Terms

  • Asset — anything of value to protect
  • Threat — potential danger to an asset
  • Vulnerability — a weakness that could be exploited
  • Risk — the likelihood/impact of a threat exploiting a vulnerability
  • Zero-day — a vulnerability with no available patch, often actively exploited

Risk Treatment Options

  • Avoid — stop the activity that causes the risk
  • Mitigate — add controls to reduce risk
  • Transfer — shift impact to a third party (insurance)
  • Accept — take no action; document the decision

Foundational Principles

Least Privilege & Need-to-know

  • Least privilege — minimum access needed for the job
  • Need-to-know — access limited to what's needed for the current task, even with clearance

Separation of Duties & Defense in Depth

  • Separation of duties — splits sensitive tasks across multiple people
  • Defense in depth — multiple overlapping layers of controls

Due Diligence vs Due Care

  • Due diligence — researching risks/requirements BEFORE acting
  • Due care — taking reasonable action based on that research
(ISC)² Code of Ethics
✓ Memorize in Order
1. Protect society, the common good, necessary public trust and confidence, and the infrastructure.
2. Act honorably, honestly, justly, responsibly, and legally.
3. Provide diligent and competent service to principals.
4. Advance and protect the profession.

When canons conflict, apply them in this order of precedence — Canon 1 always wins.
Domain 2 — Business Continuity, Disaster Recovery & Incident Response Concepts
10% of exam
▼
BCP vs DRP vs IR

Business Continuity Plan (BCP)

  • Broadest scope — keeps the overall BUSINESS running
  • Covers people, processes, facilities, and technology

Disaster Recovery Plan (DRP)

  • A subset of BCP focused specifically on restoring IT systems, data, and infrastructure
  • Answers: how do we get servers/applications back online?

Incident Response (IR)

  • Focused, tactical response to a specific security event
  • Shorter-term and narrower in scope than BCP/DRP
Business Impact Analysis (BIA) & Key Metrics
MetricDefinition
RTO (Recovery Time Objective)Maximum acceptable time to restore a system/process after disruption
RPO (Recovery Point Objective)Maximum acceptable amount of data loss, measured in time (drives backup frequency)
MTD (Maximum Tolerable Downtime)Absolute ceiling — beyond this, the business may not survive. RTO must be ≤ MTD
MTBF (Mean Time Between Failures)Average time a system operates before failing (reliability metric)
MTTR (Mean Time To Repair)Average time needed to repair a failed system and restore service
✓ Order of Planning
BIA is performed FIRST — it identifies critical functions and produces RTO/RPO/MTD targets. Those targets then drive the design of the BCP and DRP. You cannot write a meaningful recovery plan before knowing these numbers.
Backups & Redundancy

Backup Types

  • Full — copies everything; slowest backup, fastest restore
  • Incremental — changes since the LAST backup (any type); fastest backup, slower restore (needs full + all increments)
  • Differential — changes since the LAST FULL backup; restore needs only the full + latest differential

Resilience

  • 3-2-1 rule — 3 copies, 2 media types, 1 offsite/offline
  • Failover / High Availability — redundant system automatically takes over on failure
  • RAID — disk redundancy to survive a drive failure
Alternate Recovery Sites
Site TypeReadinessRecovery SpeedCost
Hot siteFully operational, real-time data replicationMinutesHighest
Warm siteHardware present, data needs restoringHoursModerate

Incident Response Lifecycle

Six Phases (in order)

  • 1. Preparation — IR plan, tools, training, contacts
  • 2. Detection & Analysis — identify that an incident occurred and its scope
  • 3. Containment — limit the damage/spread
  • 4. Eradication — remove the root cause
  • 5. Recovery — restore systems to normal operation
  • 6. Lessons Learned — post-incident review to improve defenses
⚠ Exam Trap
A common trap swaps the order — you must DETECT/ANALYZE the scope of an incident BEFORE you can properly CONTAIN it. Jumping straight to containment risks missing part of the compromise.

Authentication Factors

The Five Factor Categories

  • Something you know — password, PIN
  • Something you have — smart card, token, phone
  • Something you are — fingerprint, retina, face (biometrics)
  • Somewhere you are — geolocation, IP range
  • Something you do — behavioral biometrics (keystroke dynamics, gait)

MFA & Biometric Metrics

  • MFA = two or more DIFFERENT factor categories (two passwords is NOT MFA)
  • FAR (False Acceptance Rate) — wrongly grants an impostor access
  • FRR (False Rejection Rate) — wrongly denies a legitimate user
  • CER (Crossover Error Rate) — point where FAR = FRR; lower CER = better system

Physical Access Controls

Common Physical Controls

  • Mantrap — two interlocking doors; prevents tailgating
  • Bollard — stops vehicle-based attacks
  • Turnstile — allows one person through at a time
  • Fencing / lighting / CCTV / guards — deterrent and detective layers
  • Badge reader / smart card — something-you-have physical credential

Social Engineering Against Physical Controls

  • Tailgating / Piggybacking — following an authorized person through a secured door
  • Shoulder surfing — visually observing credentials being entered
  • Dumpster diving — retrieving sensitive discarded documents
Logical Access & Identity Management

Core Concepts

  • ACL — list defining who may access an object and what they may do
  • PAM — vaults/monitors highly privileged accounts (admin, root)
  • Provisioning/Deprovisioning — timely account creation and, critically, IMMEDIATE disabling upon termination

SSO & Federation

  • SSO — authenticate once, access many systems; convenient but a single point of compromise
  • Federation — one org's Identity Provider is trusted by a partner Service Provider, without duplicate accounts

Accountability Principles

  • Least privilege — minimum access for the job
  • Need-to-know — access limited to the current task
  • Separation of duties — no single person completes a sensitive process alone

TCP vs UDP

TCP

  • Connection-oriented — three-way handshake (SYN, SYN-ACK, ACK)
  • Reliable — guarantees delivery and order
  • Used for: web (HTTP/S), email, file transfer

UDP

  • Connectionless — no handshake
  • Faster but no delivery guarantee
  • Used for: DNS queries, streaming, VoIP

Segmentation & Architecture

Segmentation Techniques

  • VLAN — logically separates traffic and broadcast domains at Layer 2
  • DMZ — semi-trusted zone hosting public-facing services, isolated from the internal network
  • NAT — translates private internal IPs to a public IP for internet access

Zero Trust

  • Core principle: "never trust, always verify"
  • No implicit trust based on network location (inside vs outside)
  • Every request is authenticated, authorized, and continuously validated
Wireless Security & Common Threats

Wireless Protocols

  • WEP — broken; do not use
  • WPA2 — strong, widely deployed (AES-CCMP)
  • WPA3 — current standard; stronger handshake (SAE), resists offline attacks
  • Hiding SSID broadcast ≠ real security (security through obscurity)

Common Network Attacks

  • DoS — single source floods/overwhelms a target
  • DDoS — many distributed sources (botnet) overwhelm a target
  • On-path (MITM) — attacker intercepts traffic between two parties
  • Rogue access point — unauthorized AP connected to the network

Domain 5 — Security Operations
18% of exam

Data States & Data Security

Three Data States

  • Data at rest — stored on disk/database; protect with encryption
  • Data in transit — moving across a network; protect with TLS/VPN
  • Data in use — actively processed in memory; hardest state to protect

Data Classification

  • Typical levels: Public → Internal → Confidential → Restricted
  • Higher classification = stricter handling, encryption, and access controls
Cryptography Basics

Symmetric Encryption

  • Same key encrypts and decrypts
  • Fast — used for bulk data (e.g., AES)
  • Key distribution is the main challenge

Asymmetric Encryption

  • Public/private key pair — mathematically related
  • Slower — used for key exchange and digital signatures
  • Encrypt with the recipient's public key; only their private key decrypts

Hashing

  • One-way — cannot be reversed to the original input
  • Verifies integrity — same input always produces the same output
  • Any change to input produces a completely different hash
Logging, Monitoring & Security Operations

SOC & SIEM

  • SOC — team/facility that continuously monitors, detects, and responds to threats
  • SIEM — aggregates and correlates logs across systems for centralized visibility
  • Audit logs support accountability and post-incident investigation

Event vs Incident

  • Event — any observable occurrence (a login, a file access)
  • Incident — a confirmed event that negatively impacts CIA and requires response
Configuration, Change & Vulnerability Management

Operational Hygiene

  • Configuration baseline — approved, secure standard configuration
  • Change management — review/approve/test/document changes before production
  • Patch management — timely fixes reduce exposure to known vulnerabilities

Policies & Awareness

  • Acceptable Use Policy (AUP) — defines permitted use of company systems
  • Data retention policy — how long data is kept and how it's destroyed
  • Security awareness training — helps all staff recognize phishing/social engineering

Tip — IAAA happens in a fixed order
Identification → Authentication → Authorization → Accounting.

You claim who you are, prove it, are granted permissions, and your actions are logged. The exam loves to swap authentication and authorization in a scenario — remember authentication proves identity; authorization decides what you can do AFTER that identity is proven.
Trap — Control category vs control type
Every control has BOTH a category (Technical, Administrative, Physical) AND a type (Preventive, Detective, Corrective, Deterrent, Compensating, Directive). A single control can be labeled with both — e.g., a security camera is Physical (category) + Detective (type). Questions often ask for just one axis — read carefully which one is being asked.
Tip — (ISC)² Code of Ethics precedence order
When two canons conflict, the LOWER-numbered canon always wins: (1) Protect society/infrastructure, (2) Act honorably/legally, (3) Provide diligent service, (4) Advance the profession. If a scenario asks "what should you do first," protecting society and the public trust outranks everything else.
Trap — RTO vs RPO (most-confused pair on the exam)
RTO = how long can the system be DOWN (time to restore service).
RPO = how much DATA can you afford to lose (drives backup frequency).

Memory aid: RPO looks backward in time (to the last good backup); RTO looks forward (how long until we're back up).
Tip — DAC, MAC, RBAC, ABAC in one line each
DAC = owner decides. MAC = labels/clearance, no discretion. RBAC = access by job role. ABAC = access by attributes (time, device, location). If the question mentions "classification levels" or "clearance," the answer is MAC — not RBAC.
Trap — Least privilege vs need-to-know vs separation of duties
Least privilege: minimum ACCESS RIGHTS needed for the job overall.
Need-to-know: access limited to what's needed for the CURRENT TASK, even for cleared/authorized users.
Separation of duties: splits a sensitive PROCESS across multiple people so no one can complete it alone.

These are related but distinct — a scenario about splitting approval/execution of a task is separation of duties, not least privilege.
Tip — IDS vs IPS
IDS = Detective, passive, alerts only, out-of-band (cannot block).
IPS = Preventive, active, inline, can block/drop traffic in real time.

If the question says "blocks the traffic automatically," the answer is IPS, not IDS.
Trap — FAR vs FRR in biometrics
FAR (False Acceptance Rate): system wrongly LETS IN an impostor — the more dangerous error from a security standpoint.
FRR (False Rejection Rate): system wrongly LOCKS OUT a legitimate user — an inconvenience, not a breach.

The Crossover Error Rate (CER) is where FAR = FRR; a lower CER means a more accurate biometric system overall.
Tip — Symmetric vs asymmetric encryption direction
Encrypt data → use the RECIPIENT'S public key (asymmetric) so only they can decrypt it with their private key.
Symmetric encryption uses ONE shared key for both encrypt and decrypt — fast, but the key must be distributed securely first.

Remember: public key = lock (anyone can lock it), private key = the only key that opens it.
Trap — Hot site vs warm site vs cold site
Hot site: fully operational, real-time data, near-instant failover — most expensive.
Warm site: hardware ready, but data must be restored — takes hours.
Cold site: empty shell (power/HVAC only) — takes days to weeks, cheapest.

Cost and recovery speed are inversely related: faster recovery always costs more.
Tip — Incremental vs differential backup
Incremental: backs up changes since the LAST backup of ANY type — smallest/fastest backup, but restore requires the full backup plus every incremental in order.
Differential: backs up changes since the LAST FULL backup only — restore only needs the full backup plus the most recent differential.
Trap — TCP vs UDP
TCP = connection-oriented, reliable, three-way handshake (used by HTTP/S, email, file transfer).
UDP = connectionless, no delivery guarantee, faster (used by DNS queries, streaming, VoIP).

If a scenario emphasizes speed over reliability (video streaming, real-time voice), the answer is UDP.
Tip — CC exam scenario keyword shortcuts
  • "Cannot deny sending a message" → non-repudiation (digital signature)
  • "Following someone through a badge door" → tailgating/piggybacking; mitigate with a mantrap
  • "Encrypt entire hard drive" → protects data AT REST
  • "Encrypt traffic between browser and server" → protects data IN TRANSIT (TLS)
  • "Blocks traffic automatically, sits inline" → IPS, not IDS
  • "Access based on classification/clearance" → MAC
  • "Only firewall rule matters, applied to everyone" → Rule-based access control
  • "Never trust, always verify" → Zero Trust
  • "Absolute limit before the business fails" → MTD
  • "First step before writing a BCP/DRP" → Business Impact Analysis (BIA)
